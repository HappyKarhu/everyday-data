People often say they put their laptop on *standby*. But what does that actually mean?

## Sleep, standby or off?

"Standby" is a word we often use when a laptop isn't being used. But it isn't a precise technical description.

A laptop can be:

- **Idle** - still running, but not actively being used.
- **Sleep** - using much less electricity while keeping the session ready to resume quickly.
- **Off** - shut down.

So when someone says, "I left my laptop on standby," we still need to ask: **what state is it actually in?**

For this investigation, I'm comparing **Sleep** with **Off**.

That's because the ENERGY STAR data gives us model-specific electricity consumption for both states.

## The data

For this investigation, I used certified data for **20 notebook computers** from the ENERGY STAR Computers Version 9.0 database.

The models come from several manufacturers and have different Sleep and Off power values.

Rather than assigning one "typical laptop" value to all computers, I kept the model-specific data and used the **median** to represent the dataset.

The median Sleep power was:

**0.80 W**

The median Off power was:

**0.30 W**

So the difference was:

**0.50 W**

## What does 0.50 W mean over a year?

Half a watt doesn't sound like much.

But the laptop isn't in Sleep for just one minute. If we leave it there for **8 hours a day**, the difference adds up over a year.

For our 20-laptop dataset, using the median values:

**Sleep:** 2.336 kWh/year

**Off:** 0.876 kWh/year

So the additional electricity from Sleep is:

**1.460 kWh per year**

That is the electricity we are interested in the extra amount used by leaving the laptop in Sleep instead of shutting it down.

## Let's picture it with a 10 W LED bulb

Electricity in kilowatt-hours can be difficult to picture.

So let's compare that **1.460 kWh** with a **10 W LED bulb** - a simple reference for low-power room lighting.

A 10 W bulb uses:

**0.010 kWh per hour**

So:

**1.460 kWh ÷ 0.010 kW = 146 hours**

That means the extra electricity from leaving the laptop in Sleep for 8 hours a day is equivalent to running a **10 W LED bulb for about 146 hours** over one year.

That's more than six full days of continuous lighting.

And this is exactly what our poll was asking about.

## What if the laptop stays in Sleep longer?

Eight hours is our overnight scenario.

But a laptop used for work can sometimes remain in Sleep mode for much longer.

For an extended **16-hour Sleep scenario**, the additional electricity becomes:

**2.920 kWh per year**

That's equivalent to:

**292 hours of 10 W LED lighting per year.**

So the answer depends on how long the laptop remains in Sleep.

| Sleep time | Extra electricity | 10 W LED equivalent |
|------------|------------------:|--------------------:|
| 8 hours/day | **1.460 kWh/year** | **146 hours** |
| 16 hours/day | **2.920 kWh/year** | **292 hours** |

Now we have a number that is a little easier to imagine.

## So, who won the bet?

The data gave us an answer.

Leaving a laptop in Sleep mode does use more electricity than shutting it down.

For our 8-hour scenario, the difference is:

**1.460 kWh per year.**

That number may not sound very impressive on its own.

But what does it look like in everyday life?

It is equivalent to running a **10 W LED bulb for about 146 hours** over one year.

That's more than **six days of continuous lighting**.

And if the laptop stays in Sleep for 16 hours a day, the difference doubles:

**2.920 kWh per year — equivalent to 292 hours of 10 W LED lighting.**

So I wouldn't call Sleep mode a huge electricity user.

But I also wouldn't call it "nothing." 😄

The important point is that a small amount of power, repeated every day, adds up.

## What does the wider research say?

There isn't one single number for how much electricity a laptop uses in Sleep mode.

ENERGY STAR evaluates computers in several operating states, including **Off, Sleep and Idle**, and the values can differ between individual models.

The ENERGY STAR Version 9.0 specification also treats Sleep and Off as different power states. Sleep is a low-power mode designed to let the computer resume quickly, while Off is the lowest power mode defined for a computer that remains connected to the electricity supply.

ENERGY STAR also recommends using power-management features so that computers enter low-power modes when they are inactive. For certified computers, the display is set to enter Sleep within 15 minutes of inactivity and the system within 30 minutes, depending on the product and its configuration.

Our 20-laptop dataset shows why using one "typical laptop" number can be misleading. Sleep power in our selected models ranged from **0.4 to 1.5 W**, while Off power ranged from **0.1 to 0.6 W**.

So the difference isn't the same for every laptop. But the takeaway remains: **Sleep is not the same as Off — and the difference can be measured.**
